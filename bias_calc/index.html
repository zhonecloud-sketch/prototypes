Parameters

When to use this method

Use it when you decide the number of games to watch in advance, you do not peek at the results, and you make one decision at the end.

Real-life example: A casino regulator announces on Monday: "We will watch exactly 10,000 spins of this wheel, then decide if it is fair." They do not look at the results until all 10,000 spins are done. This is the cleanest way to test fairness.

Do not use it if you plan to stop early when the results look suspicious. Peeking makes the false-alarm rate higher than you think.

How to use the parameters

Expected chance if the game is fair (p₀) — the number you'd see if nothing were wrong.

  • A fair coin: 0.5
  • One number on a 37-slot roulette wheel: 0.027 (1 in 37)
  • One ticket in a 1,000-ticket lottery: 0.001
  • A basketball player who normally makes 75% of free throws: 0.75

False-alarm tolerance (α) — how often you are willing to cry "bias!" when the game is actually fair.

  • 0.05 means: out of 20 fair games you test, on average 1 will look biased just by luck.
  • 0.01 means: out of 100 fair games, 1 will look biased. Stricter, but you need more records to spot real bias.
Real-life analogy: a smoke alarm with a 5% false-alarm rate goes off once every 20 times you cook toast. You accept the occasional nuisance because you want it to catch real fires. Lower α = fewer nuisance alarms but slower to react.

Chance of catching a real problem (power) — if the game truly is biased by the amount you care about, how often will your test notice?

  • 0.80: you'll catch it 80% of the time, miss it 20%.
  • 0.90: you'll catch it 90% of the time, but you need about 30% more records.
Real-life analogy: a smoke detector with 80% sensitivity catches 4 out of 5 real fires. If you want it to catch 9 out of 10, you need a better (more expensive) detector — or in our case, more samples.
What the output means
  • p-value: "If the game were truly fair, how often would we see something this lopsided just by luck?" Small p-value = surprising result = evidence of bias.
  • Verdict: if the p-value is below your α, you say "we reject fairness" (there is evidence of bias). Otherwise, "we cannot rule out fairness".
  • Required total N: how many records you would need to reliably detect a bias as large as the one you observed, given your α and power.
  • More records needed: the difference between the required N and your current N. Zero means you already have enough.

Parameters

When to use this method

Use it when you are watching results arrive one at a time and you want to stop as soon as possible — either because you found bias or because you are satisfied it is fair.

Real-life example: A live dashboard monitors an online game's random number generator. After every draw, it recomputes the statistics. If something looks very wrong, it raises an alert. This is like a doctor continuously watching a patient's heartbeat rather than waiting for a weekly test.
How to use the parameters

Why the per-check threshold must be strict

If you check after every record and shout "bias!" whenever the p-value is below 0.05, you will be wrong a lot. Here is why in plain terms:

Analogy: Roll a fair die 100 times. You will see some suspicious-looking runs — maybe five 6s in a row, or no 1s for 20 rolls. If you declare "the die is rigged!" every time you see a run like that, you will be wrong most of the time. The more often you check, the more chances you give random luck to fool you.

Per-check alert threshold — how strict each individual check should be.

  • If you want the overall false-alarm rate to be 5% and you check after every record, use 0.001 (not 0.05).
  • Rule of thumb: divide your target overall false-alarm rate by 20 to 50.
  • If you check less often (say, every 100 records), you can use a less strict per-check threshold.

Overall false-alarm goal — informational. The app displays it so you can see what you are aiming for. It does not change the calculations.

What the output means
  • Current p-value: same calculation as the one-time review, but compared against your per-check threshold.
  • Verdict: "Alert" if the p-value is below your per-check threshold. Otherwise, "continue monitoring".
  • Required total N: with the stricter per-check threshold, how many records you would need to reliably detect a bias this large. This number is bigger than the one-time review because the threshold is stricter.

Parameters

When to use this method

Use it when you want a straightforward answer to "What is the probability this game is biased?" and you want that answer to be valid at any time, without worrying about peeking.

Real-life example: A weather forecaster says "there is an 85% chance of rain today." That is more useful than a yes/no answer, and it can be updated continuously as new data arrives. The Bayesian method works the same way: it updates the probability that the game is biased after every record.
How to use the parameters

Prior — imaginary wins and losses

Think of this as the "head start" you give yourself before looking at any data. It represents your previous belief about the game.

  • 1 and 1 (default): "I have no opinion. The game could be anything." This is the most neutral starting point.
  • 10 and 10: "I strongly believe the game is fair, as if I had already seen 10 wins and 10 losses." Use this if you have prior experience with this exact game.
  • 1 and 5: "I already suspect it leans toward losses." Use this if you have a reason to be suspicious.
Analogy: Imagine you are about to taste a new dish. If you have never had it before, you start with no expectation (1 and 1). If your friend told you it is delicious, you start with a positive expectation (10 and 10). The dish still needs to prove itself with real taste, but your starting point shifts.

Confidence needed to stop — how sure you want to be before you act.

  • 0.95: "95% sure before I call it biased." Like "beyond reasonable doubt" in a civil court.
  • 0.99: "99% sure before I call it biased." Like "beyond reasonable doubt" in a criminal court. Slower but safer.
  • 0.90: "90% sure." Faster decision, but more likely to be wrong.
What the output means
  • Posterior: your updated belief after seeing the data. It combines your prior with the real wins and losses.
  • Posterior mean: the best single estimate of the true probability.
  • 95% credible interval: the range where the true probability most likely sits. If the interval does not contain your p₀, that is strong evidence of bias.
  • P(bias toward W): the probability that the true chance of a win is above p₀.
  • P(bias toward L): the probability that the true chance of a win is below p₀.
  • Verdict: "Stop" if either probability exceeds your confidence threshold.
